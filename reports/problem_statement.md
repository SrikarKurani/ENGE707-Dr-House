Problem Statement:

Prediction of outcomes in patients at the end of life is one of the most important problems in modern health care. Clinicians frequently must estimate the likely prognosis of a critically ill patient to guide the intensity of treatment, discussions with families, and planning of end-of-life care. If these are wrong, it can mean over-treatment and unnecessary suffering, or undertreatment and the loss of lives that might have been saved. A data-driven model that supports clinical judgment on risk of mortality (not replacing it) has direct, practical value in hospital settings.

This project concerns the prediction of in-hospital mortality risk for seriously ill, hospitalised patients based on clinical, physiological and demographic data collected at or near hospital admission.

Motivation:

The uncertainty of prognosis can be stressful for patients and also place extra pressure on health services, especially in intensive care and emergency situations. A model that could predict patients at higher risk of death earlier may assist clinicians to have more informed and timely discussions with patients and their families about treatment options such as decisions around resuscitation or palliative care. This is similar to the sort of decision support that the original SUPPORT study sought to improve.


Background:

Support (Study to Understand Prognoses and Preferences for Outcomes and Risks of Treatments) was a large observational study of seriously ill adults in several major teaching hospitals in the US. Data collected in the study included physiological and functional status of patients, demographic data, and survival data. Primarily, the goal was to enhance the prediction of patient outcomes and assist with better end-of-life care decisions in hospitals.


Stakeholders:

Clinicians: They use risk estimates to prioritise care and guide challenging conversations.
Patients and families: They receive clearer, more consistent prognostic information to inform treatment decisions.
Hospital administrators: They can use overall risk data to help decide how to allocate hospital resources and plan the capacity needed for palliative and critical care.
Researchers and policy analysts: They can use this data to investigate differences in patient outcomes and treatment levels across groups, such as differences based on race or income.
Practical relevance:

Predicting risk of death is a well established use of machine learning in healthcare, but it is still very useful in practice. Even small improvements in forecasting accuracy over standard clinical scoring systems can make an important difference in patient care especially when applied to large patient populations . But these models also raise major ethical and fairness issues. For example, there are questions to be asked about whether race or income and other factors should be included in a clinical risk model or whether they should be excluded to avoid potential bias. These issues will be examined in greater depth in the later phase of this project.
